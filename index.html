The problem

The bottleneck is not the recording. It is who reads it, and for how long.

Epilepsy diagnosis depends on catching interictal spikes in EEG. The recordings exist. The specialist hours to read them do not.

Not enough readers

Specialist neurological care in India sits in the major cities. District and tier 2 hospitals often have EEG machines but no one trained to interpret the recordings.

The WHO African region has a median of 0.1 neurological specialists per 100,000 people. High income countries have 7.1, roughly seventy times as many.

WHO and World Federation of Neurology, Neurology Atlas, 2017

Not enough time

A first routine EEG caught IEDs or seizures in only 11% of adults after a first unprovoked seizure. A 24 hour ambulatory recording reached 72%, but that is 24 hours of signal someone has to read.

Hernandez-Ronquillo et al., Neurology: Clinical Practice, 2023

Interictal activity follows multiday cycles, most often 20 to 30 days long. A single short EEG is a snapshot at a random point in that cycle. Long term monitoring captures what short recordings miss by design.

Baud et al., Nature Communications, 2018

Even experts disagree. Eight expert readers scoring 13,262 candidate waveforms agreed on individual discharges with a κ of only 0.49. Artifacts mimic spikes, and morphology varies widely across patients.

Jing et al., JAMA Neurology, 2020

The goal is not to replace the epileptologist. It is to make sure their hours go to the recordings that matter.

Our approach

A clinician reviews minutes instead of hours

Epiread screens the full recording automatically and surfaces only the segments most likely to contain spikes. The operating point is chosen for the reading room, not the leaderboard.

A low false alarm rate is the point

A detector that raises dozens of false alarms per hour creates more work than it saves. At one per hour, every flag is worth a look, and the output fits a real clinical workflow without alert fatigue.

Trained on clinical data, not benchmarks

All training data comes from a tertiary epilepsy centre: real patients, real noise, real artifacts. No clean room recordings that fall apart on ward data.

Every detection is explainable

The model reports which channels and temporal features drove each detection, so the reviewer knows where to look and can override with confidence.

Built for the settings that need it most

Compact enough to run in resource constrained hospitals. The epilepsy burden is highest where specialist access is lowest, and the model is sized for that.

How it works

From a raw recording to a short review list

Every recording goes through the same three stages before a clinician sees it.

  1. 01

    Load the recording

    Recordings arrive as EDF or FIF from any 10–20 setup. Channels are mapped to the 19 standard electrodes and rebuilt into bipolar and average reference montages.

    EDF · FIF · 19 electrodes
  2. 02

    Screen every window

    The signal is bandpass filtered, notched for mains hum and resampled. The detector scores each window and keeps only those above its operating threshold.

    0.5 to 45 Hz · 50/60 Hz notch · 128 Hz
  3. 03

    Review what matters

    Flagged segments open in a paging viewer next to the original annotations. The epileptologist confirms or rejects each one, and the decision stays clinical.

    Flag at p ≥ 0.78 · 5 to 60 s pages

Questions

What people ask first

Does Epiread replace the epileptologist?

No. It decides what gets looked at first. Every flag is reviewed by a clinician, who confirms or rejects it, and the diagnosis stays with them.

Is it approved for clinical use?

Not yet. Epiread is in development and is being prepared for review by CDSCO as Software as a Medical Device. Until then it is for research and evaluation only.

Which recordings does it work with?

EDF and MNE FIFF recordings with any channel naming. Channels are mapped to the 19 electrodes of the 10–20 system, and recordings that are already montaged run directly.

What hardware does it need?

Ordinary CPU hardware. The model is small enough to run without a GPU, which matters for the hospitals that need it most.

Who built it?

An epileptologist and a signal processing engineer in India, working from clinical EEG recorded at a tertiary epilepsy centre and annotated by specialists.
